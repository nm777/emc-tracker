Understanding This Data

What is EMC?

Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC) is the moisture percentage wood will naturally absorb or release until it balances with the surrounding air. It is calculated from two weather station readings — relative humidity and temperature — using the Hailwood-Horrobin equation, the standard formula published in the USDA Forest Products Laboratory Wood Handbook (FPL-GTR-282), Chapter 4.

Why it matters for construction

Wood expands as it absorbs moisture and contracts as it dries. A 1% change in EMC causes roughly 1% dimensional change across the grain in flat-sawn lumber. When the environment swings wildly — for example from 97% humidity at dawn to 32% by afternoon — the wood is subjected to stress it cannot immediately respond to. Over time this causes warping, checking, cupping, joint failure, and finish defects.

In a humidity-controlled environment (HVAC, dehumidifiers), EMC typically stays within 1–2% on a given day. The colored bars below the chart show how far outside that range each day actually was, proving whether or not the environment was controlled.

Daily Swing Risk Levels

Safe ≤ 2%

High 5–10%

Extreme > 10%
Severe environmental failure. Rapid moisture cycling will cause visible defects in wood products.

How the numbers are calculated

Hourly relative humidity and temperature data is sourced from Open-Meteo.com (historical weather station archive). For each hour, EMC is computed using the Hailwood-Horrobin two-hydrate model. Each day is then summarized into four values: minimum hourly EMC, maximum hourly EMC, the average across all hours, and the swing (max minus min) representing the total moisture stress the wood experienced that day.

Validation

Run node validate.js to verify the EMC formula against 25 reference points from USDA FPL Wood Handbook Table 4-2. The formula is accurate to within ±0.6% of the published table values.
